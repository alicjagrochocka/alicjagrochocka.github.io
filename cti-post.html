Cyber Threat Intelligence: How Financial Institutions Track Adversaries

April 28, 2026

Most people assume cybersecurity is primarily a technical discipline — firewalls, patches, detection rules. But inside a major financial institution, one of the most critical functions is something closer to intelligence work than engineering. The Cyber Threat Analysis (CTA) team exists to answer a deceptively simple question: which threats actually matter to us, right now?

What is threat intelligence — and what it isn't

Intelligence has a precise definition that matters here. It is the product of collection, processing, integration, evaluation, analysis, and interpretation of available information. The key words are analysis and assessment. Intelligence is a written document or brief that interprets information to inform decisions that lead to an action.

This means the following are not intelligence:

  • Indicator of Compromise (IOC) feeds — these are data points
  • Raw logs — also data points
  • Any data or information presented without context

An IP address flagged in a feed tells you nothing by itself. Intelligence tells you that a specific threat actor group has been using that IP to target the SWIFT infrastructure of European banks over the past 30 days, with a 70% success rate against institutions lacking MFA on privileged accounts.

The role of the CTA team

At a high level, the CTA team sifts through all available reporting to determine which cyber threats present the most significant risk to the firm. This is harder than it sounds. The volume of threat reporting from vendors, government partners, and open sources is enormous — and most of it is irrelevant to any given institution's specific environment, assets, and risk profile.

The firm's Threat Intelligence Standard shapes CTA's work around several objectives:

  • Enable decisions about cybersecurity controls based on specific threat actor TTPs
  • Guide collection toward threats posing the highest risk to the firm
  • Ensure intelligence meets standards for timeliness, relevance, and usability
  • Increase sector-wide preparedness and help actively detect and block threats
  • Support law enforcement efforts to deter cybercriminals in the financial sector

The concept of Priority Intelligence Requirements (PIRs) is central here. These are the critical questions leadership needs answered first — the intelligence gaps that, if left unfilled, would leave decision-makers flying blind on the most consequential risks. Collection Management is then the process of directing resources to answer those questions.

TTPs: how attackers operate, not just what they use

CTA teams focus heavily on Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs) — the behavioral fingerprints of threat actors. This is a deliberate choice. Indicators like IP addresses and file hashes change constantly; a sophisticated adversary rotates infrastructure daily. TTPs are far more durable. A threat actor's preferred method for achieving persistence on Windows systems won't change just because they got a new server.

Intelligence on TTPs flows through a structured feedback loop:

01
TTPs Identified — CTA identifies a new attack technique from vendor reports, open sources, or partner sharing
02
Hunt Team Verifies — The hunt team tests whether existing controls detect or respond to that TTP in the firm's actual environment
03a
Controls work ✓ — TTPs are handed off to Detection Engineering to write formal detection rules
03b
Controls don't work ✗ — CTA re-assesses the threat level; the risk rating is updated and leadership is notified of the detection gap

A concrete example: LockBit targeting financial institutions

CTA reads a CrowdStrike report indicating LockBit is using a scheduled task to maintain persistence after initial access into financial firms:

$taskPrincipal = New-ScheduledTaskPrincipal -UserId 'CORPORATE_DOMAIN\user01' -RunLevel Highest

The hunt team goes into the firm's environment and simulates this technique. If the EDR flags it, Detection Engineering writes a formal rule: "Alert whenever a scheduled task is created with RunLevel Highest by a non-admin user." If the EDR misses it, LockBit's threat rating goes up and there's now a documented gap in the firm's defenses.

One threat intel report on a ransomware group either produces a new detection protecting the bank, or raises an alarm that existing defenses are insufficient. That's the bridge between intelligence and defensive operations.

MITRE ATT&CK: the shared language

The MITRE ATT&CK Matrix is the industry-standard taxonomy for mapping and sharing threat intelligence. Updated at least annually with industry input, it covers platform-specific matrices for Windows, macOS, Linux, cloud, and mobile environments.

CTA teams populate and maintain these matrices as new intelligence is discovered. The matrix creates a common language that allows intelligence to be shared across institutions, sectors, and with law enforcement — you can describe a threat actor's behavior in a way that any security team globally can immediately understand and act on.

Information sharing: the financial sector's network effect

No single institution can see the full threat landscape. Adversaries who fail against one bank often pivot to another. The financial sector has built formal sharing structures to address this:

  • FS-ISAC (Financial Services Information Sharing and Analysis Center) — the primary sector-wide intelligence sharing hub
  • ARC (Analysis and Resilience Center for Systemic Risk) — focused on systemic risk to the financial system
  • Government partners — including law enforcement and national cybersecurity agencies

The dark web as an intelligence source

Perhaps the most underappreciated intelligence source for financial sector analysts is the dark web. This is where adversaries operate openly — selling tools, sharing techniques, and sometimes announcing attacks before they happen.

Category What analysts find
Stolen credentials Bank login dumps, employee credentials for sale
Ransomware announcements Groups like LockBit posting new victims before public disclosure
Malware for sale Banking trojans (Emotet, TrickBot) sold in criminal forums
Threat actor chatter Forums where criminals discuss targeting specific institutions
Data leaks Customer PII, card numbers posted after breaches
Initial Access Brokers Criminals selling existing VPN/RDP access into bank networks

The operational value is significant. A ransomware group posting "we have access to Bank X" before an attack happens gives the target an early warning window. Credential dumps of employee emails allow forced password resets before attackers can use them. And tracking specific threat actor groups over time — their language, targets, and tools — builds the kind of behavioral profile that makes attribution possible.

The bigger picture

What makes a financial institution's CTI program effective is the same thing that makes any intelligence program effective: the discipline to distinguish between data and analysis, the organizational structures to act on what the analysis reveals, and the humility to recognize that the adversary is also learning.

The CTA team's job is not to collect everything — it's to tell decision-makers what matters, why, and what to do about it. That's a fundamentally different problem from building detection rules or patching vulnerabilities, and it requires a fundamentally different kind of thinking.
